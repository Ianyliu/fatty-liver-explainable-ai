\section{Introduction}
An ultrasound examination contains several views of a patient's anatomy. Their diagnostic information can differ because of orientation, tissue coverage and image quality. A model that combines these views can use information unavailable in any single frame, but its patient-level prediction leaves an important question unanswered: which images support the prediction, and which contribute little or oppose it?

\citet{yen2024fattyliver} developed a grouped-ultrasound graph neural network (GNN) for fatty-liver classification. Images form the nodes of a graph, similarity between their representations defines the edges, and aggregation produces an examination-level prediction. This representation accommodates variable numbers of images and allows related views to exchange information. Our objective is to explain the resulting prediction at the same interpretable scale as the input: the individual ultrasound image.

Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) approximates a complex prediction function through perturbations expressed in interpretable variables \citep{ribeiro2016lime}. Whereas conventional image explanations perturb superpixels, we perturb the set of images belonging to one examination. Each subset is passed through the grouped-image model, and a binary inclusion matrix links its response to the images it contains. A regularized linear surrogate then summarizes how inclusion of each image is associated with the prediction. Pearson correlations provide a corresponding marginal summary. Together, these quantities make both image ordering and the direction of association visible without retraining the GNN.

The choice of perturbations is central to this approach. Random subsets can repeatedly receive the same predicted class, leaving little information about the model's behavior across the classification boundary. We therefore propose two-stage Adaptive Class-Balanced Sampling: an initial random stage is followed by sampling that favors images individually predicted to belong to the underrepresented class. The aim is to broaden the responses encountered during surrogate fitting. Whether that broader coverage also improves approximation is an empirical question, which we examine separately from whether the resulting image rankings track changes in model output.

The contribution is an image-subset explanation framework and its evaluation in grouped liver ultrasound data. We assess sampling behavior, held-out probability fidelity, deletion of ranked images, and agreement across repeated fits and single-image omissions. Related approaches include additive feature attribution \citep{lundberg2017shap} and graph-specific subgraph explanations \citep{ying2019gnnexplainer}; our focus is the statistical role of the image-inclusion design and the interpretation of its fitted coefficients.

\section{Image-subset explanation framework}
\subsection{Grouped prediction and the inclusion design}
Let $\mathcal X_i=\{x_{i1},\ldots,x_{in_i}\}$ denote the ultrasound images for patient $i$. The model encodes each image using DenseNet121 \citep{huang2017densenet}, connects images with correlated feature representations, and aggregates their information through graph attention \citep{velickovic2018gat}. Mean pooling produces a representation of the examination, from which the model returns two class probabilities. The trained model is held fixed while its inputs are perturbed.

For an inclusion vector $\boldsymbol u\in\{0,1\}^{n_i}$, define the retained set and its probability response as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal X_i(\boldsymbol u)=\{x_{ij}:u_j=1\},\qquad
q_i(\boldsymbol u)=\Pr_{\mathrm{GNN}}\{\text{class }1\mid G_i(\boldsymbol u)\},
\label{eq:response}
\end{equation}
where $G_i(\boldsymbol u)$ is reconstructed from the retained images. Rebuilding the graph allows an omission to affect both the information supplied by that image and the relations among the remaining views. Class 1 is the model's fatty-liver class.

For $B$ sampled subsets, the interpretable design and response vector are
\begin{equation}
U_i=(u_{rj})_{B\times n_i},\quad
u_{rj}=\mathbf 1\{x_{ij}\in\mathcal X_i(\boldsymbol u_r)\},\quad
\boldsymbol q_i=\{q_i(\boldsymbol u_1),\ldots,q_i(\boldsymbol u_B)\}^{\mathsf T}.
\label{eq:design}
\end{equation}
Each column represents an image and each row an examination subset. This converts the explanation problem into regression on a patient-specific binary design. Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} shows how sampling, grouped prediction and image-level summaries fit together.

The grouped-image model supplies responses, and regularized regression and correlation summarize conditional and marginal image influence.}\label{fig:workflow}\end{figure}

\subsection{Two-stage adaptive sampling}
Random sampling first draws subset size $K$ uniformly from $\{3,\ldots,n_i\}$ and then selects $K$ distinct images uniformly without replacement. Varying size exposes the surrogate to both small collections of views and nearly complete examinations. Images are distinct within a subset, while subsets can recur across draws.

Adaptive sampling uses the same size distribution and a budget of $B=1{,}000$ draws, with a target of 500 subset predictions in each class. Stage I samples randomly until one class reaches its quota. Stage II then favors the singleton-predicted image pool associated with the deficient subset class. Specifically, a deficient class-1 quota requests a positive-pool fraction of $0.85$; a deficient class-0 quota requests $0.15$. These fractions determine integer pool counts, rather than image-selection weights. If $p_i$ and $m_i$ are the positive- and negative-pool sizes, the selected positive count is
\begin{equation}
c_i(K,\rho)=\min\{p_i,\ \max[\lfloor\rho K\rfloor,\ K-m_i]\},
\qquad \rho\in\{0.15,0.85\}.
\label{eq:pool}
\end{equation}
The remaining $K-c_i$ images are sampled from the negative pool. This rule preserves subset size by reallocating a short pool's deficit. If either pool is empty, sampling remains random. Appendix~\ref{app:sampling} gives the sequential rule in full.

The intuition is to use image-level predictions to direct subset generation toward a less frequently observed response. Because grouped predictions combine views through a graph, singleton classes guide sampling rather than fixing a subset's class. We record both the realized pool composition and the resulting prediction balance to distinguish how the sampler operates from what it achieves.

\subsection{Conditional and marginal image influence}
A linear surrogate assigns each image a coefficient on the model's probability scale:
\begin{align}
\widehat q_i(\boldsymbol u)&=\widehat b_i+\boldsymbol u^{\mathsf T}\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}_i,\\
(\widehat b_i,\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}_i)&=
\arg\min_{b,\boldsymbol\beta}
\left\{\|\boldsymbol q_i-b\boldsymbol 1-U_i\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2
+\lambda\|\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2\right\},\quad\lambda=1.
\label{eq:ridge}
\end{align}
Ridge shrinkage \citep{hoerl1970ridge} moderates variation in the fitted coefficients across finite perturbation samples. The intercept is unpenalized and inclusion indicators retain their common binary scale. Within the surrogate, $\widehat\beta_{ij}$ is the change in predicted response associated with adding image $j$ while holding the other inclusion indicators fixed. Its sign distinguishes supportive from opposing associations; its magnitude summarizes their strength on the probability-response scale.

Elastic Net combines an $\ell_1$ penalty, which can set coefficients to zero, with an $\ell_2$ penalty, which shrinks coefficients together \citep{tibshirani1996lasso,zou2005elasticnet}. It therefore offers a potentially sparser explanation than Ridge. Five-fold cross-validation on training subsets selects its penalty and mixing parameter. The precise objective and tuning grid appear in Appendix~\ref{app:regression}.

Pearson correlation, $r_{ij}=\operatorname{Cor}(U_{i\cdot j},\boldsymbol q_i)$, instead summarizes the marginal association between image inclusion and the response. It asks whether subsets containing that image tend to receive higher probabilities, without adjustment for which other images they contain. This differs from the conditional question answered by regression. In particular, under random sampling, an inclusion indicator is positively associated with subset size: $\operatorname{Cov}(U_j,K)=\operatorname{Var}(K)/n_i$. Thus, when output depends on the number of retained images, marginal association can reflect both image content and examination size. Appendix~\ref{app:marginal} derives this identity and the binary-predictor correlation formula.

\section{Data and evaluation design}
\subsection{Ultrasound data}
The data originated from Taiwan Biobank \citep{feng2022taiwan}; ultrasound examinations were acquired at the MacKay Memorial Hospital Health Examination Center in Taipei, as described by \citet{yen2024fattyliver}. We analyzed 135 patients with positive observed liver-fat labels, at least 20 metadata-listed images and complete image availability in the supplied test split. Their 3,072 images comprised 20--35 views per patient. The unit of explanation was the patient examination.

For each patient, we generated 1,000 training subsets under each sampling scheme using three seeds. Ridge, Elastic Net and Pearson summaries were obtained from these designs. Independent evaluation subsets and deletion controls were shared across the two sampling schemes so that their comparisons were paired. Software, preprocessing and additional design diagnostics appear in Appendix~\ref{app:software}.

\subsection{Fidelity on held-out subsets}
Fidelity asks how accurately an interpretable surrogate approximates the GNN on subsets that did not contribute to fitting. We generated 200 random evaluation draws per patient and seed, then retained masks absent from both training designs. Both surrogates were evaluated on this same multiset, retaining repeated evaluation draws. For sampling scheme $a$ and seed $s$, the error was
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{MAE}_{isa}=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal E_{is}|}\sum_{\boldsymbol u\in\mathcal E_{is}}
\left|q_i(\boldsymbol u)-\widehat q_{isa}(\boldsymbol u)\right|,
\label{eq:mae}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal E_{is}$ denotes the retained evaluation draws. Lower MAE indicates better approximation. Each surrogate was also compared with a constant equal to its own training-mean response, which establishes whether image-specific predictors improve on an examination-level average.

We averaged seed-specific values within each patient and then weighted patients equally. Paired differences compared adaptive with random sampling for the same patient. Reported standard deviations describe variation across patient means. Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation} specifies aggregation and metric availability.

\subsection{Deletion and repeatability}
Probability approximation and image ordering answer different questions. To examine the ordering, we removed images in descending order of their signed Ridge coefficients and compared the resulting probability trajectory with ascending and random-order deletion. If the largest positive coefficients identify supportive views, their removal should lower class-1 output sooner than either control. Deletion proceeded to nominal fractions $0,0.1,\ldots,0.5$; every retained set was passed through graph reconstruction and prediction. We integrated each trajectory over the actual deleted fractions and compared AUCs within patient and seed. A negative descending-minus-control contrast indicates that descending deletion produced the lower trajectory.

We also assessed whether explanations persisted across perturbation samples. Three seed pairs per patient were compared using rank correlation, overlap of the five highest-ranked images, and sign agreement. Finally, leave-one-image-out (LOO) effects measured the change from the complete examination when each image was omitted. Comparing those effects with the fitted rankings connects the broad subset-based explanation to behavior near the full examination. Detailed definitions and supplementary results appear in Appendices~\ref{app:evaluation} and~\ref{app:additional}.

\subsection{Sampling coverage and surrogate fidelity}
Adaptive sampling changed the range of responses represented in the training designs. The mean class-1 proportion decreased from \RandomPositivePercent{}\% under random sampling to \AdaptivePositivePercent{}\% under adaptive sampling, and the adaptive proportion was closer to $0.5$ in \BalanceCloser{} of \PrimaryN{} patients. Both classes occurred in \RandomBothClasses{} of \PrimaryRuns{} random designs and \AdaptiveBothClasses{} adaptive designs. Exact balance was uncommon, however: only \TargetSuccesses{} adaptive run reached 500 predictions per class. The realized sampling diagnostics in Table~\ref{tab:sampling} and Figure~\ref{fig:sampling} help explain this difference between improved coverage and quota attainment. Reallocation was needed in \DeficitPercent{}\% of Stage II draws, and adaptive designs also contained more repeated masks.

The constant comparison places these errors in context. Random-trained Ridge improved on its training-mean constant in \RandomBeatsConstant{} of \PrimaryN{} patients, although its overall mean MAE was lower than the constant's \RandomConstantMAE{}. Thus, the aggregate improvement coexisted with many examinations for which an image-independent prediction was competitive. Adaptive-trained Ridge improved on its own constant in \AdaptiveBeatsConstant{} patients, but that constant was less accurate overall (MAE \AdaptiveConstantMAE{}). Because each constant reflects a different training distribution, these counts describe gains within each design rather than a reversal of the random-versus-adaptive comparison.

Elastic Net produced mean MAEs of \RandomEnetMAE{} and \AdaptiveEnetMAE{} under random and adaptive sampling, respectively. Relative to Ridge, this reduced absolute probability error by \RandomEnetGainPP{} and \AdaptiveEnetGainPP{} percentage points. These small differences suggest that sparse regularization changed probability approximation less than the sampling design did. \subsection{Do the rankings track model behavior?}
Descending deletion lowered class-1 output more rapidly than ascending or random deletion under both training schemes (Figure~\ref{fig:deletion}). Random-trained rankings yielded mean AUCs of \RandomDeletionDescending{} for descending, \RandomDeletionAscending{} for ascending and \RandomDeletionRandom{} for random deletion. The corresponding adaptive-trained values were \AdaptiveDeletionDescending{}, \AdaptiveDeletionAscending{} and \AdaptiveDeletionRandom{}. Descending-minus-random contrasts averaged \RandomDeletionDelta{} and \AdaptiveDeletionDelta{}, and were negative in every patient for both schemes (Table~\ref{tab:deletion}). The ordering therefore identified supportive images under sequential removal even though the linear surrogates differed substantially in probability fidelity.

\section{Discussion}
Image-subset perturbation brings grouped-ultrasound predictions into an interpretable regression design. Whole views, rather than pixels or latent features, become the explanatory variables. This preserves the scale at which an examination is acquired and reviewed, while making the sampling distribution an explicit part of the explanation. The resulting coefficients summarize the model across the retained combinations of views; correlations offer a simpler marginal description of those same combinations.

The evaluation reveals a distinction between obtaining diverse training responses and approximating them accurately. Adaptive sampling increased representation of the deficient predicted class, but its fitted surrogate had higher error on held-out random subsets. The evaluation subsets followed the random scheme, so random training more closely matched the distribution on which fidelity was measured. Adaptive training shifted image co-inclusion patterns to seek rarer responses. Such a shift can expose useful behavior while making one additive approximation less accurate over the random-subset distribution. The observed pool shortages further reduced the requested Stage II bias, helping explain why coverage improved without reliably attaining balance.

Regularization and sampling addressed different statistical problems. Shrinkage moderated coefficient variation, and Elastic Net offered sparse fits, but neither removed interactions created by feature aggregation and graph reconstruction. Importantly, the inclusion designs were full rank and had moderate condition numbers. Similarity among ultrasound views should therefore not be equated with severe collinearity of the binary design. It can instead appear as redundancy in the prediction function: several views may supply overlapping information, so omitting one changes little until other supportive views are also removed.

That distinction helps reconcile the fidelity and deletion findings. A surrogate can preserve which images are more supportive without reproducing the entire nonlinear probability surface. Deletion tests that ordering along a trajectory starting from the complete examination, whereas held-out MAE assesses probability values across many subset sizes. The negative deletion contrasts and the agreement with LOO effects support the usefulness of the rankings for examining this model's response. They do not imply that the additive fit is equally accurate throughout the subset space.

Marginal and conditional summaries should likewise be read according to the questions they answer. Because including any image increases expected subset size under the random design, a positive marginal correlation can partly reflect a response to having more images. Conditional coefficients account for the other inclusion indicators, giving a different comparison. This makes panel-to-panel sign differences informative rather than contradictory. Conditioning marginal summaries on subset size, or reporting size-stratified associations, would further clarify this component of the explanation.

\subsection{Limitations and further work}
The study concerns positive-label examinations and one supplied model checkpoint; it does not assess classification accuracy across positive and negative patients. Results are descriptive, and repeated seeds quantify within-patient repeatability rather than independent patient replication. The cohort includes examinations inspected during preliminary analysis. Adaptive sampling required additional singleton predictions, and one random deletion order per seed provides limited characterization of the control distribution. Novel-mask filtering can preferentially exclude frequently recurring, nearly complete subsets, so fidelity reflects the retained subset distribution.

Linear surrogate predictions can lie outside $[0,1]$, and near-constant responses can leave rank comparisons undefined. The original illustrative image panels retain classifier-scale coefficients and source uncertainty markings; their exact fit-to-panel provenance has not been established, and these markings were not used for inference in the probability-response evaluations. The supplied model used an edge threshold of $0.95$, compared with $0.995$ in the source publication. Finally, the explanations describe model behavior; clinician review and external validation are needed to establish diagnostic relevance, and causal image importance is not identified by this analysis.

Further work should test perturbation designs under matched total-query budgets, evaluate fidelity near the complete examination, and compare size-stratified or interaction-aware surrogates. A probability-constrained response model could address out-of-range predictions. Blinded clinician review would assess whether the selected views correspond to recognizable diagnostic evidence.

\section{Conclusion}
Image-subset perturbations provide marginal and conditional explanations of grouped-ultrasound GNN predictions. Adaptive Class-Balanced Sampling broadened prediction-class coverage, while random sampling gave better probability fidelity on held-out random subsets. Both designs produced rankings whose descending deletion lowered model output relative to controls. These findings show why response coverage, probability approximation and image ordering should be evaluated together when interpreting multi-image predictions.
